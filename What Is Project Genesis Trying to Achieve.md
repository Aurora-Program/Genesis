

Perhaps one of the main mistakes I made when I began working with artificial intelligence was believing that the dimensions of its vectors were not semantic—that their values did not, in some way, represent properties of reality.
The nature of these representations remains a subject of debate in the philosophy and science of artificial intelligence. Nevertheless, it is becoming increasingly difficult to maintain that current models possess no semantic understanding whatsoever.
The fundamental point is simple: if their internal representations contained no semantic structure, artificial intelligence would be unable to understand relationships, generalize, or reason. It would merely repeat, like a parrot, combinations it had encountered before.
But that is not all we observe.
Current systems can construct new analogies, apply a relationship learned in one context to a different one, and reach conclusions that no human may ever have formulated in exactly the same way. We can debate what kind of reasoning they perform, or how closely it resembles human reasoning, but it is becoming less convincing to claim that they are merely repeating.
My conclusion is that current models do possess semantic representations. The problem is that they contain this semantics at an extremely low density.
To understand this, we can use another well-known numerical vector: RGB.
In the RGB system, each color is represented by three values: red, green, and blue. Each dimension has a known function, and by combining the three, we can represent an enormous range of colors.
RGB is efficient because it was not discovered by randomly accumulating colors. It was designed using our knowledge of how colors can be operationally produced from three components. We know what each axis represents and how the axes must be combined.
Now imagine that we did not know about those components. Imagine trying to construct a color space by performing millions of experiments and observing which colors tended to appear near one another.
We would probably end up creating a vector with twenty or thirty dimensions. Some would be redundant, several combinations would produce almost identical results, certain regions of the color spectrum would be represented better than others, and calculating each color would require far more operations than necessary.
The system would contain chromatic information, but its chromatic density would be very low.
Something similar happens with embeddings.
Because we do not know in advance what the fundamental dimensions of meaning are, models discover them indirectly by observing enormous quantities of language. A word acquires its representation from the contexts in which it appears and from its relationships with other words.
The proximity between words is not arbitrary: it has grammatical, functional, thematic, and semantic causes. By analyzing millions of associations, the model manages to construct a space that represents genuine aspects of meaning.
Semantics therefore exists within these representations. But it is distributed, entangled, and fragmented across hundreds or thousands of continuous dimensions. An individual dimension usually tells us very little; it is the interaction among many dimensions that allows a meaningful property to be recovered.
This representation has proven extraordinarily powerful, but it is also expensive. To represent a word, a relationship, or an idea, the system must store and operate on large vectors of floating-point numbers. To reason with them, it must repeat these operations across many layers, continually comparing representations whose semantic information is highly dispersed.
The cost of reasoning increases because the system does not operate directly on explicit relationships. Instead, it operates on distributed patterns from which those relationships must be reconstructed again and again.
This is where Project Genesis begins.
Genesis does not start from the assumption that embeddings are meaningless. On the contrary, it begins with the recognition that they contain valuable semantic knowledge discovered through an immense statistical exploration.
The proposal is to distill that knowledge:
distributed pattern → discovered relationship → ordered structure → reusable synthesis.
This is not simply a matter of reducing a vector with thousands of dimensions to a smaller one. Conventional dimensionality reduction could eliminate precisely the relationships that need to be preserved.
The goal is to transform a statistical, continuous, and distributed representation into semantic tensors that are explicit, hierarchical, and verifiable. In Aurora, these structures are organized through Form, Function, and Structure: FO, FN, and ES.
The objective is for every component to carry a much greater amount of useful semantic information, while allowing the context to activate only the dimensions relevant to the operation being performed. The tensor can remain stable while its specific function emerges from its position and its relationships with other tensors.
If we can represent meaning using far fewer dimensions—and if those dimensions possess a much higher semantic density—the result will not merely be a reduction in memory requirements.
It could also radically reduce the amount of work required to reason.
A relationship that has already been discovered would not need to be statistically reconstructed every time. It could be preserved as a reusable structure, applied in new contexts, and verified by re-executing it.
That is the true ambition of Genesis: to transform the distributed knowledge discovered by current models into a smaller architecture capable of preserving and reproducing its essential relationships.
Neural models would have carried out the great initial exploration of semantic space. Genesis would attempt to organize their discoveries.
It does not seek to demonstrate that current models do not understand. It seeks to understand how they understand, extract the structures they have discovered, and transform them into a much more efficient, explicit, and shareable form of reasoning.
The fundamental shift would be from an intelligence that requires enormous amounts of computation to reconstruct meaning to an intelligence capable of operating directly on densely semantic structures.
It would not merely be a compression of vectors.
It would be a compression of reasoning itself.
